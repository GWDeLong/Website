Motivation

Before we discussed how to find the likelihood of an events occurring when those events had no influence over one another. Now we talk about the other case; we ask 'What happens when events are dependent on one another?'

Dependent and Conditional Probability

First, we define dependency.

Definition (Dependent Probability).

Two or more events are called dependent if the occurrence of one event influences the other events.

The computation of these probabilities is very similar to the dependent case, however we introduce the conditional. 

Definition (Conditional Probability).

The probability of event \( A \) happening, given that event \( B \) has already happened.

We denote this probability as

\[ P( A \mid B ) \]

read "The probability of \(A\), given \(B\)."

The Classical Example

Likely the most common example of dependence is the bag of marbles example.

Suppose we have a bag of colored marbles, when we pick a marble, we remove that marble from the bag. The composition of the bag is given by the table below.

Marble Bag Contents
Marble Color Count
Red 5
Blue 2
Green 3

We want to know the probabilities of drawing certain marbles.

What is the probability of drawing a blue marble from the bag?

As before, we know that the probability of a single event is the quotient of the successes and the total number of possibilities, therefore 

\[ P(\text{Blue}) = \frac{2 \text{ Blue Marbles}}{10 \text{ Total Marbles}}. \]

Now, after taking the blue marble out, what is the probability of drawing a green marble?

This is the conditional probability that we discussed specifically the probability of drawing a green, given a blue has been drawn. We find

\[ P(\text{Green} \mid \text{Blue}) =  \frac{3 \text{ Green Marbles}}{9 \text{ Total Marbles}}\begin{matrix}\\ \rightarrow \text{ The total minus the one removed} \end{matrix}. \]

Indeed, we find that the probability has changed.

Dependent AND

We compute the probability of two dependent events \( A \) and \( B \), one occurring after the other as the product of first probability and the conditional of the second, i.e., 

\[P( A \text{ AND } B) = P(A)\cdot P(B \mid A). \]

Below are some examples of the dependent AND.

Example.

Suppose we have a bag of colored marbles with the following composition.

Marble Bag Contents
Marble Color Count
Black 10
Blue 5
Red 3
Green 2

If we draw marbles with removal (rather than replacing them), what is the probability of drawing a black marble first and a blue marble second?

Applying our rule

\begin{align*}
P(\text{Black AND Blue}) &= P(\text{Black})\cdot P(\text{Blue} \mid \text{Black}) \\
&= \dfrac{10 \text{ Black}}{20 \text{ Total}}\cdot\dfrac{5 \text{ Blue}}{19 \text{ Total}} = \dfrac{50}{380}
\end{align*}

Therefore the probability of drawing a black and a blue marble is 5/38 or approximately 13.16%.

Note.

The AND operation does not change with the order of statements, this is because of the commutativity of multiplication, particularly in the denominator, for instance we observe

\[ P(\text{Blue AND Black}) = P(\text{Blue})\cdot P(\text{Black} \mid \text{Blue}) = \frac{5}{20}\cdot\frac{10}{19} = \frac{5}{38} = P(\text{Black AND Blue}). \]

We also ask what happens when we have multiple AND statements. Our answer, we follow the same pattern, returning to our previous example, we find

\[ P(\text{Black AND Blue AND Green}) = \frac{10 \text{ Black}}{20 \text{ Total}}\cdot\frac{5 \text{ Blue}}{19 \text{ Total}}\cdot\frac{2 \text{ Green}}{18 \text{ Total}} = \frac{100}{6080}. \]
