Motivation

Now that we have the idea of distributions, we want to understand where the data is in a distribution.

The Standard Deviation

To simplify our notes, we consider renaming the mean to a variable.

Notation.

The mean of a data set is denoted by the lowercase Greek letter pronounced "mu" in English and written as

\[ \mu \]

Continuing in the direction of the Greeks we introduce the following definition.

Definition (Standard Deviation).

The standard deviation is the average distance of all data points from the mean. We use the lowercase Greek letter pronounced "sigma" and written as

\[\sigma\]

For our purposes within this class, we will always be given the standard deviation of a data set. The equation -- which is not necessary for you to remember -- for computing the population standard deviation is given below for the sake of interest.

\[ \sigma = \sqrt{\dfrac{\sum_{i = 1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2}{n - 1}},\]

where \( n \) is the number of samples, \( x_i \) is the \(i\)-th data point, and \(\mu\) our mean. 

Review: Percentages

Due to the Empirical rule having to do with percentages, it is in our best interest to quickly review the topic. We first look at conversions. For our examples we will work with the number 123.456 and convert between percentage and decimal and vice-versa.

Converting Percentages to Decimals.

To convert a percentage to a decimal, we move the decimal point two place values to the left, if there are no numbers to the left, we write zeroes in their place.

\[ 123.456\% \text{ is written as } 1.23456 \text{ in decimal form.} \]

Converting Decimals to Percentages.

To convert a decimal to a percentage, we move the decimal point two place values to the right, if there are no numbers to the right, we write zeroes in their place.

\[ 123.456 \text{ is written as } 12345.6\% \text{ in percent form.} \]

Now we look at using our conversion to understand what a percent of a number is.

Finding a Percentage of a Number. 

Suppose we wish to find 75% of 90, we would compute the percentage as follows

\[ 75\% \text{ of } 90 = (0.75)90 = 67.5 \]

We simply multiply the number by the decimal expression of the percentage.

The Empirical Rule

Also known as the "68-95-99" rule, the rule states the following.

Given that a distribution is normal (symmetric about the mean), we know that

  • 68% of the data can be found within one standard deviations of the mean.
  • 95% of the data can be found within two standard deviations of the mean.
  • 99.7% of the data can be found within three standard deviations of the mean.

We present the rule as a graph to help understand the above.

Let's take a look at an application of the Empirical Rule.

Suppose we ask 39 students what their height is and their results are normal, we record the following information

\begin{align*}
&\text{The mean of the data} &&\mu = 66\text{in or } 5'6'' \\
&\text{The standard deviation of the data} &&\sigma = 4\text{in}
\end{align*}

Where "in" is the unit for inches and \(x'y''\) is read \(x\) foot, \(y \) inches.

Now applying The Empirical Rule and recalling we had a total of 39 students, we find

  • 68% of the class. or \( (0.68)39 = 26.52 \to 26 \) people are between \( 5'2'' \) and \( 5'10'' \).
  • 95% of the class, or \( (0.95)39 = 37.05 \to 37 \) people are between \( 4'10'' \) and \( 6'2'' \).
  • 99.7% of the class, or \( (0.997)39 = 38.883 \to 38 \) people are between \( 4'6'' \) and \( 6'6'' \).

Note that we are rounding down because a fraction of a person is nonsensical.
