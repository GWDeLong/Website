Motivation

Previously we worked with data sets, we now want to know the chance of something happening.

Probability

We begin with a definition

Definition (Probability).

The likelihood of an event occurring.

Notation.

The probability of event \(A\) occurring is denoted as \( P(A)\), a number between 0 (0%) and 1 (100%).

We compute the probability of a single event happening as

\[ \text{Probability} = \frac{\text{Successful Outcomes}}{\text{All Possible Outcomes}}, \]

Example.

What is the probability of rolling a 2 on a six-sided dice?

The possible outcomes are

\[ 1 \quad \underline{2} \quad 3 \quad 4 \quad 5 \quad 6 \]

noting that 2 only occurs once and there are 6 possibilities we find

\[ P(2) = \frac{1}{6}. \]

We now consider how to deal with multiple events occurring at once. We look at the connectives AND and OR, where AND carries the requirement that both events happen and OR carries that either event would happen.

Independence

For now, we consider the independent case.

Definition (Independent Probability).

Two events are called independent if one outcome has no influence on the other outcome.

As example, if we flip a coin and roll a dice, or rolling two dice. Either of which are two events happening with no interference from each other.

Independent AND

We compute the probability of two independent events \( A\) and \( B \) both occurring as the product of their probabilities, i.e.,

\[P(A \text{ AND } B) = P(A) \cdot P(B). \]

Below are some examples of computing AND probabilities.

Example.

What is the probability of rolling a two (2) on a six-sided dice AND a heads (H) on a fair coin?

As before, we can list every possible outcome, and select the successful ones

\[ \begin{matrix} 1H  & \underline{2H}  & 3H  & 4H  & 5H  & 6H \\ 1T  & 2T  & 3T  & 4T  & 5T  & 6T   \end{matrix} \] 

one success over twelve possibilities yields \( P(2 \text{ AND } H) = 1/12 \).

Our brute force method is limited by the possibilities, so instead we use our previous formula \( P(A \text{ AND } B) = P(A)\cdot P(B) \), therefore

\begin{align*}
P(2 \text{ AND H}) &= P(2) \cdot P(H) \\
&= \frac{1}{6} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{12}.
\end{align*}

Thus there is a one in twelve chance of rolling a two and flipping a heads.

Example.

What is the probability of rolling an even number (EVEN) on a six-sided die and a tails (T) on a fair coin?

Listing outcomes and finding successful ones 

\[ \begin{matrix} 1H  & 2H  & 3H  & 4H  & 5H  & 6H \\ 1T  & \underline{2T}  & 3T  & \underline{4T}  & 5T  & \underline{6T}   \end{matrix} \] 

Therefore

\[ P( \text{EVEN AND T}) = \frac{3}{12} = \frac{\cancel{3} \cdot 1}{\cancel{3} \cdot 4} = \frac{1}{4}.\]

Instead, using our formula

\begin{align*}
P( \text{EVEN AND T}) &= P(\text{EVEN}) \cdot P(\text{T}) \\
&= \frac{3}{6} \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{3}{12} = \frac{1}{4}.
\end{align*}

Before we continue to the OR statement, we need to review how to add fractions.

Review: Fraction Addition

 Let \(a, b, c\), and \(d\) be numbers, then we add the following factions as such.

\begin{align*}
\frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} &= \frac{d}{d} \cdot \frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d}\cdot \frac{b}{b} \tag{Finding a common denominator of \( bd \)}\\
&= \frac{ad}{bd} + \frac{bc}{bd} \tag{Simplifying} \\
&= \frac{ad + bc}{bd} \tag{adding the numerators}
\end{align*}

To lessen the reliance of variables we work on a more concrete example of adding 4 and 1/3, the addition follows

\begin{align*}
\frac{4}{1} + \frac{1}{3} &= \frac{3}{3} \cdot \frac{4}{1} + \frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{1}{1} \\
&=  \frac{12}{3} + \frac{1}{3} \\
&= \frac{13}{3}.
\end{align*}  

Independent OR

We compute the probability of two independent events \( A\) or \( B \) occurring as the sum of their probabilities subtracted by the probability of them both occuring, i.e.,

\[P(A \text{ OR } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P( A \text{ AND } B). \]

The subtraction isn't an obvious part of the formula. In Probability, we consider what's called "probability spaces" or universes in which \( A \) or \( B \) can happen, visualized below

We note that if we consider \( A + B \), we would be count the intersection twice, hence the need to subtract.

Now turning to our examples.

Example.

 What is the probability of rolling an even number (EVEN) on a six-sided die or a tails (T) on a fair coin?

Applying our formula

\begin{align*}
P(\text{EVEN OR T}) &= P(\text{EVEN}) + P(\text{T}) - P(\text{EVEN AND T}) \\
&= \frac{3}{6} + \frac{1}{2} - \frac{3}{6}\cdot\frac{1}{2} \\
&= \frac{2}{2}\cdot\frac{3}{6} + \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{6}{6} - \frac{3}{12} \\
&= \frac{6}{12} + \frac{6}{12} - \frac{3}{12} \\
&= \frac{6 + 6 - 3}{12} = \frac{9}{12} = \frac{3}{4}.
\end{align*}

therefore there is a three out of four chance to roll a even number or flip a tails.

Example.

 What is the probability of rolling a two (2) on a six-sided dice or a heads (H) on a fair coin?

Our answer

\begin{align*}
P(\text{2 OR H}) &= P(\text{2}) + P(\text{H}) - P(\text{2 AND H}) \\
&= \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{6}\cdot\frac{1}{2} \\
&= \frac{2}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{6}{6} - \frac{1}{12} \\
&= \frac{2}{12} + \frac{6}{12} - \frac{1}{12} \\
&= \frac{2 + 6 - 1}{12} = \frac{7}{12}.
\end{align*}

So there is a seven in twelve chance of rolling a 2 or flipping a heads.
