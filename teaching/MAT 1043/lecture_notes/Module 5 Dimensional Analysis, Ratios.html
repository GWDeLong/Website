Motivation

Before we can talk about the main topic of Module 5, Cost of Living, it is necessary to review some concepts which may have fallen out of familiarity, those being ratios and dimensional analysis. 

Ratios

One of the most common pieces of mathematics which we deal with on the daily is the ratio. 

Definition (Ratio).

A relative size of value, denoted \( A \colon B, A / B\), or \( \frac{A}{B} \), read as "\( A\) to \( B \)", "\( A\) over \( B \)", or "\( A\) out of \( B \)", 

We note that ratios may be expressed as decimals, e.g., \( 1/2 = 0.5 \). In addition, the numerator \( A \) may be greater than \( B \), this would simply be a larger ratio.

Some ratios we see in daily life are the following.

  • Nine out of ten (\( 9/10 \)) dentists agree that toothpaste works.
  • The Cowboys beat the Commanders thirty seven to twenty (\( 37 \colon 20 \)) on September 20th, 2026.

We will use ratios in our next topic.

Dimensional Analysis (Unit Conversions)

In general, a unit is better described through examples, but we offer a rather lack luster definition to begin the idea.

Definition (Unit).

A unit is an amount of some quantity.

Some examples are as follows.

  • Weight (Pounds - lbs, Grams - g, etc.)
  • Distance (Feet - ft, Meters - m, etc.)
  • American (Football Fields - FFs, Washing Machines - WMs, etc.)

The last group of units is very common in the news and also when talking about hail (golf ball size, orange size, etc.).

We now turn to the process of using dimensional analysis to convert units, we will build a table with different units cancelling along the diagonals and connecting different units via known ratios as described after the implementation.

\[ \begin{matrix} X \cancel{\text{ Unit }A} & \Big| & Y \text{ Unit }B \\
\hline & \Big| & Z \cancel{\text{ Unit }A} \end{matrix}
= \dfrac{X \cdot Y }{Z} \text{ Unit }B \]

Some known unit ratios are

\begin{align*}
1 \text{ foot (ft)} &= 12 \text{ inches (in)} \\
1 \text{ mile (mil)} &= 5280 \text{ feet} \\
1 \text{ yard (yd)} &= 3 \text{ feet} \\
1 \text{ football field (FF)} &= 100 \text{ yards (yds)}
\end{align*}

Some other ratios are

\begin{align*}
1 \text{ cup} &= 8 \text{ ounces (oz)} \\
1 \text{ quart (qt)} &= 4 \text{ cups} \\
1 \text{ gallon (gal)} &= 4 \text{ quarts (qts)} \\
\end{align*}

As a bonus we may consider the ratio

\[ 1 \text{ 1043 instructor (teacher)} = 5'10 = 70 \text{ in}. \]

Using Dimensional Analysis

Suppose we want to know how many 1043 instructors would be the same distance as a football field. We will start with what we know (the football field) and end with the ratio that we want (how many instructors). Between we will use our known ratios to connect what we know to what we want.

\[ \begin{matrix} 1 \text{ FF} & \Big| & 100 \text{ yds} & \Big| & 3 \text{ ft} & \Big| & 12 \text{ in} & \Big| & 1 \text{ teacher}  \\
\hline & \Big| & 1 \text{ FF} & \Big| & 1 \text{ yd} & \Big| & 1 \text{ ft} & \Big| & 70 \text{ in} \end{matrix}
= \dfrac{100 \cdot 3 \cdot 12 }{70} \approx 51.43 \text{ teachers}\]

Hence 

\[ 1 \text{ football field } \approx 51 \text{ teachers}. \]

A much more useful example is as follows. 

Suppose we are cooking and using a digital scale which displays only ounces. We are using a recipe that calls for three and one half (or 3.5) cups of flour, We want to know how many ounces are in 3.5 cups of flour, using dimensional analysis we find

\[ \begin{matrix} 3.5 \text{ cups} & \Big| & 8 \text{ oz} \\
\hline & \Big| & 1 \text{ cup} \end{matrix}
= 3.5 \cdot 8 = 28 \text{ oz}.\]
